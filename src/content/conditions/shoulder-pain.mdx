## Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)

Frozen shoulder is also called adhesive capsulitis. They are the same condition, not two different ones. The main problem is stiffness: the arm will not go further, even when someone else moves it for you. It is hard to reach behind your back or above your head, in every direction.

It usually affects people aged 40 to 60, more often women, and is more common with diabetes or thyroid problems. It moves through three stages: freezing, frozen and thawing. Full recovery can take up to 3 years. Most frozen shoulders improve without surgery.

## Rotator cuff problems

The rotator cuff is a group of muscles and tendons that hold the upper end of the arm bone in the shoulder socket and help you lift and turn the arm. A rotator cuff problem causes pain on lifting the arm, and severe pain or catching while lowering it after lifting. The arm may feel weak, and pain at night is common, especially lying on that side.

It is seen most often in middle-aged women, many of them homemakers, who do a lot of overhead work and lift heavy things at home, or who fall onto the shoulder. It is also common in people who lift overhead repeatedly, such as racquet players and gym-goers. A tear can get larger over time. Many rotator cuff problems get better with physiotherapy and other non-surgical care.

Not sure which one you have? Read [frozen shoulder or rotator cuff?](/guides/frozen-shoulder-or-rotator-cuff)

## Shoulder dislocation

A dislocation means the ball at the upper end of the arm bone has come out of the shoulder socket. It usually happens with a fall or a sudden force, such as a tackle. The shoulder is very painful, may look out of shape, and you cannot move the arm. Go to an emergency department. Do not try to push it back yourself.

After a first dislocation, the shoulder can come out again, especially in young, active people. A follow-up review helps plan rehabilitation and decide whether anything more is needed.

## How we decide on treatment

The plan depends on the person. The main questions are whether the problem is stiffness, weakness or instability, how much it affects your sleep, work and daily life, and what you want to get back to. Not every case needs surgery. Many shoulder problems improve with physiotherapy, exercise and time. When surgery is advised, it is often done by keyhole (arthroscopic) surgery, and I will explain why it is the right choice for you.
